# GDP

## Summary

Gross Domestic Product (GDP) is a primary indicator of a nation's economic health, representing the total value of all goods and services produced within a country. It serves as a benchmark for measuring economic growth, standard of living, and the overall performance of a country's economy. It is used to evaluate national convergence toward regional averages (e.g., EU income levels) and to assess the transition from low-cost growth models to innovation-driven economies.

## Key Details

- **Economic Growth Indicator:** Used to track the expansion or contraction of an economy over time (e.g., projected rebounds in Hungary's GDP growth to 0.8% in 2025 and 2.5% in 2026).
- **Fiscal Context:** Often used as a denominator to measure public debt (e.g., Hungary's public debt at 73.5% of GDP in 2024) and to monitor budget deficits (e.g., a deficit reaching 5.3% of GDP triggering excessive deficit procedures).
- **Investment and Productivity:** GDP is influenced by labor productivity levels, R&D spending (which contributes to long-term growth), and the volume of domestic and foreign investment.
- **Structural Vulnerabilities:** GDP figures can be affected by high inflation, cost competitiveness, and the regulatory environment which may distort market functioning.
- **Economic Outturn:** GDP serves as the baseline for reporting annual economic outturns and assessing the impact of private consumption and investment on national growth.
- **Convergence and Growth Drivers:** In emerging economies, GDP growth is increasingly analyzed through the lens of shifting from low labor costs to high-tech investment, infrastructure modernization, and R&D intensity (e.g., Romania's current R&D intensity at 0.52% of GDP).
- **Fiscal Dynamics (Poland Case Study):** GDP is used to monitor budget deficits; for instance, a deficit reaching 5.3% of GDP can trigger excessive deficit procedures, necessitating medium-term fiscal-structural plans to reduce the ratio (e.g., targeting below 3% by 2028).
