# Media Pluralism

## Summary

Media pluralism refers to the diversity of media outlets, ownership structures, and journalistic voices within a democratic society. It encompasses regulatory independence, public broadcaster governance, economic sustainability, and legal protections for journalists. Recent EU Rule of Law assessments highlight divergent national trajectories: Austria and Belgium demonstrate ongoing structural reforms and regulatory adaptation, whereas Hungary exhibits systemic challenges with minimal progress. Italy presents a mixed picture: while its regulator AGCOM maintains independence and stable funding, and digital concentration rules were modernised, governance reforms for public broadcaster RAI lag, and legal restrictions on judicial information access alongside defamation law amendments raise concerns. Furthermore, journalists across these regions face escalating threats, with Italy notably reporting surveillance via spyware and persistent SLAPPs. Malta presents a critical risk landscape where the government is preparing legislation to expand the media regulator's oversight of market mergers and state advertising, though editorial independence of public service media remains stagnant. Access to official documents is restricted, and while a cross-border SLAPP law has been enacted, broader journalist protections are pending. The unresolved assassination of journalist Daphne Caruana Galizia continues to cast a long shadow over media safety and anti-corruption efforts. Poland demonstrates active legislative recalibration, drafting reforms to implement the European Media Freedom Act to restore regulator independence and streamline licensing. While politically biased content in public service media has decreased, editorial independence remains debated amid the liquidation of state broadcasters and a planned transition from licence fees to direct budget funding. Challenges persist regarding media ownership transparency and journalist safeguards, though a decrease in strategic lawsuits has accelerated legislative work on anti-SLAPP directives and source protection. In Romania, no progress has been made on securing editorial independence for public service media. The National Audiovisual Council (CNA) faces resource constraints, while opaque political advertising and state funding continue to threaten journalistic independence. Transparency of media ownership remains a high risk, and threats or harassment against journalists persist, alongside growing concerns over Strategic Lawsuits Against Public Participation (SLAPPs).

## Key Details

- **Regulatory Independence**: Bodies such as KommAustria and RTR maintain operational independence in overseeing media standards and market compliance. Italy's AGCOM similarly operates independently with stable funding, adhering to EU mandates. Malta is preparing legislation to expand its media regulator's oversight of market mergers and state advertising, though independence remains under scrutiny. Conversely, Hungary's National Media and Infocommunications Authority (NIMASA) lacks functional independence, raising concerns over impartial oversight and compliance with EU mandates. In Romania, the National Audiovisual Council (CNA) continues to face significant resource constraints, limiting its capacity to effectively safeguard media pluralism and enforce regulatory standards.
- **Public Broadcaster Governance**: The ORF governance structure was reformed following a Constitutional Court ruling to enhance its autonomy and reduce political influence. In Italy, RAI secured a licence fee reversal, though comprehensive governance reforms continue to lag. Belgian community-specific broadcasters (VRT, RTBF, BRF) also face governance complexities and notable budgetary constraints, including a funding freeze for RTBF. In Malta, no progress has been made on ensuring the editorial independence of public service media. Securing editorial independence for public service media in Romania remains stalled with no measurable progress. In contrast, Hungarian public service media remains financially dependent on the governing majority and heavily subject to political influence.
- **Funding & Economic Landscape**: Targeted funding for quality journalism yields positive outcomes in some regions, but the private media sector faces deteriorating economic conditions globally. State advertising expenditures have doubled in Austria, raising concerns regarding market distortion. Malta is introducing legislation to regulate state advertising, but broader economic safeguards for independent media remain pending. Italy has modernised digital media concentration rules to address market consolidation and prevent undue influence. In Hungary, state advertising continues to heavily favor pro-government outlets, skewing the competitive landscape. Opaque political advertising and state funding mechanisms in Romania continue to pose significant threats to journalistic independence, exacerbating financial vulnerabilities for independent outlets.
- **Transparency & Access to Information**: Preparations for Austria's Freedom of Information Act are on track for a September 2025 launch. Belgian legislative updates have improved access, though procedural fragmentation limits effectiveness. Italy faces challenges with restrictions on accessing judicial information and amendments to defamation laws that impact free press operations. Malta continues to restrict access to official documents, limiting public scrutiny of media and government funding. In Hungary, access to information remains severely restricted, particularly concerning public funding allocations and media subcontractors. Romania faces high risks regarding media ownership transparency, complicating efforts to ensure accountability and prevent hidden political influence in the media landscape.
- **Journalist Safety & Legal Protections**: Safety measures continue to evolve despite persistent verbal attacks, with strategic lawsuits against public participation (SLAPPs) remaining relatively low in Austria. Belgium has decriminalised defamation and proposed anti-SLAPP transpositions, yet journalists report increasing harassment and political interference. Italy reports escalating threats, legal harassment (SLAPPs), and surveillance via spyware, with protective measures proving insufficient. Malta has enacted a law targeting cross-border SLAPPs, but broader protections for journalists remain pending. Hungarian journalists face intensified pressure, including coordinated smear campaigns, selective denial of press access, and investigations by the Sovereignty Protection Office under allegations of serving "foreign interests." The unresolved assassination of journalist Daphne Caruana Galizia in Malta continues to significantly impact the investigative climate and safety standards for media professionals across the region. In Romania, threats and harassment against journalists persist, accompanied by growing concerns over Strategic Lawsuits Against Public Participation (SLAPPs), undermining press freedom and investigative reporting.
- **Hungarian Media Landscape & Civil Society Pressures**: The 2025 Rule of Law Report notes no measurable progress in media pluralism. Beyond regulatory capture and state advertising bias, independent media operate under heightened scrutiny, with access to information restricted regarding public funding and subcontractors. Civil society space is further obstructed by sovereignty protection mechanisms targeting independent operations.
- **Belgian Regulatory & Public Broadcast Structure**: Community-specific regulators (Flanders, French-speaking, German-speaking) maintain independence while adapting to EU mandates like the Digital Services Act and European Media Freedom Act.
- **Legal Safeguards & Journalist Harassment (Belgium)**: Defamation has been decriminalised and anti-SLAPP transposition is proposed. However, journalists report increasing harassment, political interference, and intimidation online and during demonstrations.
- **Access to Public Documents (Belgium)**: Legislative updates have improved access, though procedural fragmentation and non-binding appeal bodies limit effectiveness.
- **Poland**: Drafting reforms to implement the European Media Freedom Act aim to restore transparency and independence to broadcasting regulators and streamline licensing procedures. Politically biased content in public service media has notably decreased, though editorial independence remains debated amid the liquidation of state broadcasters and a temporary suspension of licence fee funding. The state plans to transition public broadcasting to direct budget funding. Challenges persist regarding media ownership transparency and safeguarding journalists, although a notable decrease in strategic lawsuits has accelerated legislative work on anti-SLAPP directives and protections for journalistic sources.
