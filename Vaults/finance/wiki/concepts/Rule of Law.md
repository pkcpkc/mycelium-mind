# Rule of Law

## Summary

The Rule of Law constitutes a comprehensive framework for evaluating the structural and operational integrity of a jurisdiction's legal and political systems. Recent comprehensive assessments, notably the 2025 European Commission reports on member states such as Austria, France, Italy, and Slovakia, demonstrate that while baseline metrics like judicial independence and anti-corruption perception scores often remain stable, critical systemic gaps frequently undermine full compliance with European standards. The evaluation typically spans four interconnected pillars: judicial efficiency and prosecutorial autonomy, anti-corruption transparency and enforcement, media pluralism and regulatory independence, and institutional checks alongside civic space resilience. Continuous monitoring highlights the necessity of legislative reforms, structural separation of powers, and robust implementation of supranational human rights obligations.

## Key Details

- **Judicial System & Prosecutorial Autonomy**: High perceived judicial independence often coexists with misalignments in appointment procedures for key court leadership roles. Structural reforms are frequently required to insulate prosecutorial offices from political influence, reduce administrative reporting burdens, and address systemic understaffing to maintain case efficiency. Recent implementations, such as France's 2023 magistrate status reform, introduce changes like repealed age limits and 360° evaluations for court presidents. The Superior Council of Magistracy actively defends independence against personal attacks and political pressure. Digitalisation efforts (e.g., Portalis) aim to improve efficiency, though implementation speed and technical issues persist. High average resolution times for cases (e.g., bribery, family law) and backlogs remain persistent challenges, alongside delays in implementing European Court of Human Rights judgments. Cross-jurisdictional assessments similarly highlight the High Council for the Judiciary executing new oversight mandates following comprehensive reforms, though proposed constitutional separation of judges and prosecutors faces significant judicial pushback. Staffing shortages and technical hurdles continue to delay the full digitalisation of criminal courts, while judicial efficiency shows only marginal improvement. Persistent length of proceedings remains a critical concern under Council of Europe supervision. **Recent Slovakia Assessment (2025)**: Proposed amendments to judges' laws aim to improve court efficiency but raise concerns regarding the Judicial Council's expanded role and insufficient safeguards for dismissing its non-judge members. The Prosecutor General's power to annul final prosecutorial decisions continues to raise independence concerns, compounded by the dissolution of specialised anti-corruption bodies like the Special Prosecutor's Office and National Crime Agency, which has led to investigation delays.
- **Anti-Corruption & Public Oversight**: National strategies are typically active, but enforcement mechanisms for lobbying and revolving-door policies remain limited. Effective asset declaration systems for legislators are often absent, while public procurement is consistently flagged as a high-risk sector. Whistleblower channels and transparency registers show steady improvement, though increased reporting volumes prompt calls for better support structures. In jurisdictions with low corruption perception scores (e.g., 67/100 CPI), agencies like the AFA are being reorganised to separate public and private sector functions, while oversight bodies like HATVP expand resources for asset declarations. Prosecution efforts target organised crime through enhanced PNF and OCLCIFF funding, and new ethics measures for parliamentarians and magistrates are being systematically rolled out. Recent assessments note that while national anti-corruption plans are updated and oversight agencies like ANAC expand capacity, public perception of corruption remains high. Constitutional changes, such as the abrogation of the abuse of public office offence, have been upheld, yet corruption convictions have declined. Comprehensive lobbying transparency and unified political party financing registers remain stalled in several jurisdictions, while public procurement vulnerabilities persist due to organised crime infiltration. **Recent Slovakia Assessment (2025)**: High perceived corruption levels persist, exacerbated by 2024 criminal law reforms that shortened statutes of limitations and reduced penalties, directly impacting ongoing high-level cases. Progress on regulating lobbying, strengthening asset declarations, and reforming conflicts of interest rules remains limited. Public procurement reforms have inadvertently lowered oversight safeguards, increasing corruption risks for businesses and deterring investment.
- **Media Pluralism & Freedom**: Regulatory bodies generally maintain operational independence, and public broadcaster governance reforms enhance editorial autonomy. However, economic pressures on private media, combined with significant increases in state advertising, raise concerns about market distortion and political proximity. Legislative frameworks like Freedom of Information Acts are critical for transparency, though journalists frequently report practical barriers to accessing official documents. Recent developments include expanded regulatory scopes under frameworks like the DSA and EMFA, public broadcasting consolidation debates (e.g., France Médias) sparking autonomy concerns and strikes, and new bills addressing neighbouring rights to ensure fair compensation for press content on digital platforms. In other jurisdictions, regulators maintain independence and secure stable funding, while public broadcasters navigate licence fee reversals amid stalled governance reforms. Digital media concentration rules have been modernised, but access restrictions to judicial information and amendments to defamation laws raise free press concerns. Journalists increasingly face threats, legal harassment (SLAPPs), and surveillance via spyware, with existing protective measures proving insufficient. **Recent Slovakia Assessment (2025)**: Establishment of a new public service broadcaster entity has sparked concerns over political influence and editorial independence. Amendments to Freedom of Information Acts have introduced potential paywalls for extensive information requests and extended response times. Journalist safety has deteriorated, marked by increased verbal attacks, SLAPPs, and abandoned plans for defamation law reform.
- **Institutional Checks & Civic Space**: Independent authorities face ongoing scrutiny regarding politicisation, prompting commitments to transparent, merit-based appointments. Implementation rates for supranational human rights judgments serve as a key compliance metric, alongside regulatory and tax reforms that expand eligibility for civil society organisations. Legislative simplification efforts are welcomed but often draw criticism for rushed consultation processes and accelerated procedures. The financial environment for civil society organisations may appear stable, yet civic space frequently narrows due to intimidation and attacks. Emerging technologies, such as AI in the justice system, require careful oversight to ensure appropriate safeguards and compliance with human rights standards. Constitutional reforms aimed at altering executive selection methods (e.g., direct prime ministerial elections) advance amid criticism over excessive use of urgency decrees. Reforms to supreme audit institutions spark debates on administrative liability, while lagging implementation of European Court of Human Rights judgments highlights systemic accountability gaps. Efforts to establish National Human Rights Institutions frequently stall, and newly enacted security legislation draws international warnings regarding potential constraints on civic space and fundamental freedoms. **Recent Slovakia Assessment (2025)**: Institutional checks face strain due to the frequent use of fast-track legislation, which systematically bypasses meaningful stakeholder consultation. The civil society environment has narrowed, marked by new reporting burdens for NGOs, funding cuts for human rights advocates, and increased political polarisation. Constitutional amendment discussions also raise questions regarding the primacy of EU law.
