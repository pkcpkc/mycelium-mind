# Anti-Corruption Framework

## Summary

The anti-corruption framework refers to the interconnected legal, institutional, and procedural mechanisms established to prevent, monitor, investigate, and remediate corrupt practices. Across EU member states, performance varies significantly based on institutional capacity, legislative transparency, and enforcement track records. Austria and Belgium demonstrate strong baseline mechanisms with effective whistleblower channels and active national strategies, though gaps remain in lobbying oversight and asset declarations. Hungary presents a contrasting landscape with high perceived corruption, operational obstacles for oversight bodies, and a lack of high-level prosecutions despite ongoing strategy implementation. Italy's 2025 assessment reveals a mixed environment: while the National Anti-Corruption Plan was updated and the National Anti-Corruption Authority (ANAC) expanded its capacity, public perception remains high and overall convictions have declined. Similarly, Malta faces significant scrutiny with high public perception of corruption; although the Financial Crimes Investigation Department and the Attorney General have expanded capacities, final convictions in high-level cases remain low, and the Permanent Commission Against Corruption operates with limited resources yielding no concrete outcomes. Poland presents a mixed trajectory where perception remains high; however, significant structural shifts are underway, including the operationalization of the European Public Prosecutor Office and the dissolution of the Central Anti-Corruption Bureau to redistribute competences. Despite active investigations into large-scale corruption and a new whistleblower protection law, Poland faces hurdles with lobbying regulations, standardized digital asset declarations, and political party financing weaknesses. Slovakia presents a distinct challenge where high perceived corruption levels persist, heavily impacted by 2024 criminal law reforms that shortened statutes of limitations and reduced penalties, directly undermining high-level case enforcement. The jurisdiction also experienced institutional fragmentation through the dissolution of specialized anti-corruption bodies like the Special Prosecutor's Office and National Crime Agency, resulting in investigation delays and a notable drop in corruption convictions. While a new National Anti-Corruption Strategy is in preparation, legislative progress on lobbying regulation, asset declarations, and conflict of interest reforms remains limited. Furthermore, while progress exists on conflict-of-interest regulations across some jurisdictions, comprehensive lobbying transparency and unified political party financing registers remain stalled. Public procurement consistently emerges as a high-risk sector, with Italy specifically noting vulnerabilities to organised crime infiltration, Malta highlighting vulnerabilities linked to investor citizenship schemes, and Slovakia experiencing increased risks due to recently lowered oversight safeguards that deter investment.

## Key Details

- **Performance & Strategy**: Austria consistently scores well on international indices, underpinned by the active implementation of its National Anti-Corruption Strategy. Belgium maintains operational cooperation between the Central Office for the Repression of Corruption (CDBC-OCRC) and prosecutors. Conversely, Hungary reports high perceived corruption and a notable decrease in final convictions despite implementing its 2024-2025 strategy. Slovakia presents a contrasting trajectory, characterized by a significant drop in corruption convictions following 2024 criminal law reforms that shortened statutes of limitations and reduced penalties. While a new National Anti-Corruption Strategy is in preparation, enforcement has been hampered by institutional restructuring. Italy shows a mixed trajectory: the National Anti-Corruption Plan was recently updated and ANAC's capacity expanded, yet public perception of corruption remains relatively high and overall convictions have declined.
- **Transparency & Disclosure Gaps**: Critical shortcomings remain in lobbying frameworks and financial transparency across the region. Austria's framework is narrowly scoped with minimal revolving-door rules and lacks mandatory MP asset declarations. Belgium's lobbying reform has stalled and its asset regime lacks independent verification. Hungary's reforms on lobbying and asset declarations remain pending or insufficient. Slovakia mirrors these regional gaps, with limited legislative progress on regulating lobbying, strengthening asset declarations, and reforming conflicts of interest. Similarly, Italy faces stalled progress on comprehensive lobbying transparency and unified political party financing registers, though it has made some strides in regulating conflicts of interest. Poland also lacks progress on introducing lobbying regulations and standardizing digital asset declarations, with impunity clauses and broad immunities for top executives continuing to hinder accountability.
- **High-Risk Sectors & Oversight**: Public procurement is explicitly flagged as a high-risk sector across all assessed jurisdictions. Belgium notes fragmented integrity policies prompting career-long screening, Austria relies on continuous oversight by national bodies, and Hungary faces risks due to fragmented oversight favoring state-aligned entities. Italy specifically highlights persistent vulnerabilities in public procurement regarding organised crime infiltration, prompting targeted oversight measures. Slovakia's public procurement sector faces elevated corruption risks after recent reforms inadvertently lowered oversight safeguards, directly deterring investment. Malta highlights vulnerabilities linked to investor citizenship schemes, while Poland continues to grapple with graft in large-scale procurement.
- **Legal & Institutional Evolution**: The legal landscape continues to shift with varied outcomes. In Italy, the constitutional abrogation of the "abuse of public office" offence was upheld, reflecting broader debates on criminalizing specific administrative misconduct. Slovakia's institutional framework underwent significant consolidation with the dissolution of specialized anti-corruption bodies, including the Special Prosecutor's Office and National Crime Agency. These functions were redistributed, centralizing prosecutorial authority under the Prosecutor General, whose expanded power to annul final decisions raises ongoing independence concerns. Austria continues to advance investigations despite political scrutiny, and Hungary's Integrity Authority faces ongoing operational data-access obstacles. Poland is restructuring anti-corruption competences by dissolving the Central Anti-Corruption Bureau and redistributing its functions to police and security agencies, while fully operationalizing the European Public Prosecutor Office.
- **Investigation Dynamics**: Investigations into high-level corruption actively proceed in Austria, though prosecutors face intense public and political scrutiny. In Belgium, allegations of information leaks and potential political interference in high-profile cases have raised public trust concerns. Both jurisdictions are advancing "follow-the-money" investigative strategies to better target financial and organised crime. Slovakia's investigation dynamics have notably slowed, with the dissolution of specialized units leading to procedural delays and a measurable decline in high-level corruption case resolutions. Poland is actively investigating large-scale corruption cases, supported by newly enacted whistleblower protection laws that provide vital channels for reporting irregularities.
- **Whistleblower Protections**: Austria's reporting channels function effectively as a critical operational component. Belgium shows limited progress, particularly regarding parliamentary whistleblower laws and cooling-off periods for senior officials. Italy's framework benefits from ANAC's expanded mandate to receive and process whistleblower reports, though public trust remains a challenge. Poland has enacted a new whistleblower protection law to bolster reporting mechanisms and safeguard against retaliation. Slovakia's framework remains under development, with the upcoming National Anti-Corruption Strategy anticipated to address reporting gaps, though current mechanisms operate within a broader environment of narrowed institutional checks.
- **Institutional Transparency Improvements**: Recent reforms in Austria have enhanced legislative transparency, notably through a public register for parliamentary groups. Belgium has implemented updates to improve access to public documents, though procedural fragmentation limits effectiveness. Slovakia's legislative environment faces additional strain from the frequent use of fast-track procedures, which systematically bypass stakeholder consultation and limit transparent policy development.
- **Malta's Jurisdictional Assessment**: The anti-corruption framework in Malta faces significant scrutiny with high public perception of corruption. While the Financial Crimes Investigation Department and the Attorney General have expanded capacities, final convictions in high-level cases remain low. The Permanent Commission Against Corruption continues to operate with limited resources and has yet to achieve concrete prosecutorial outcomes.
- **Malta's Integrity & Legal Reforms**: Integrity measures for politicians and high-level officials remain inadequate, particularly regarding asset declarations and lobbying rules. The jurisdiction recently experienced a major legal setback when the European Court of Justice ruled in April 2025 that its investor citizenship scheme violates EU law. Additionally, a reform increasing the threshold for magisterial inquiries has been criticized for hindering accountability in high-level offences.
