# Fiscal consolidation

## Summary

Fiscal consolidation refers to structured government policies aimed at reducing budget deficits and stabilizing public debt, typically implemented through targeted spending reforms, revenue base broadening, and medium-term structural plans. In recent macroeconomic assessments, it emphasizes balancing near-term flexibility to allow automatic stabilizers to function during economic downturns with a disciplined medium-term framework designed to achieve sustainable fiscal targets. Internationally, multilateral frameworks (e.g., IMF Article IV consultations) frequently recommend multi-year consolidation pathways to address deteriorating debt trajectories and structural imbalances. 

Recent analyses, such as the **2025 Country Report for [[Romania]]**, highlight fiscal consolidation as a critical tool for nations undergoing rapid economic convergence. In these contexts, consolidation is not merely about austerity but serves as a vehicle for structural transformation—moving from models based on low labor costs toward innovation-driven growth. It requires a resolute path to reduce government and current account deficits (twin deficits) while simultaneously addressing high inflation and structural vulnerabilities like R&D underinvestment and regulatory burdens.

## Key Details

- **Deficit Reduction & Debt Trajectory Management:** A primary objective is to reduce the structural fiscal deficit (e.g., below 2-3% of GDP) while managing long-term debt trajectories. Recent assessments emphasize frontloaded structural efforts and multi-year Medium-Term Fiscal Plans to reverse trajectories exceeding 100-120% of GDP. Country-specific targets, such as Poland's medium-term goal to lower the deficit from 5.9% to 2.9% of GDP by 2028, Romania's phased reduction to below 6% by 2026 and under 3% by 2030, and Slovakia's targeted reduction from 5.8% to 2.2% of GDP by 2028, illustrate how consolidation pathways are tailored to national debt ceilings and growth capacities amid widening twin deficits or structural slippage.
- **Phased Implementation Strategy:** Advises near-term fiscal flexibility to support automatic stabilizers during downside scenarios, paired with bold medium-term consolidation measures to ensure long-term sustainability and counteract slowing growth or competitiveness headwinds. Implementation often involves a deliberate short-to-medium term growth phasing, where adjustment measures cause temporary deceleration (e.g., 1.8% growth in Slovakia during 2025 consolidation) before rebounding (e.g., 2.1% in 2026) as external demand recovers and EU-funded investments take effect.
- **Institutional Transparency & Governance:** Successful consolidation increasingly requires robust institutional frameworks to build credibility and mitigate political risks. This includes establishing independent fiscal councils, improving budget transparency, accelerating stalled EU fund disbursements, enhancing public investment management, and strengthening governance frameworks for state-owned enterprises (SOEs) to prevent fiscal drains. Recent frameworks also emphasize constitutional and legislative safeguards, such as reforming national debt brakes ahead of fiscal thresholds and legally strengthening independent budgetary responsibility councils to prevent disruptive consolidation and ensure long-term debt sustainability.
- **Structural Spending & Benefit Reforms:** Prioritizes gradually lowering high pension replacement rates, rationalizing social benefits, capping or reforming public sector wage increases (including automatic wage indexation systems), enhancing healthcare system efficiency, and eliminating environmentally harmful subsidies. Recent measures, such as Romania's freeze on public sector wages and pensions, demonstrate how temporary fiscal brakes are used alongside permanent structural reforms to offset demographic pressures and improve labor force participation, particularly among women.
- **Revenue Mobilization & Tax Restructuring:** Focuses on broadening the tax base through strategic increases in property, inheritance, gift, and excise taxes, alongside a gradual shift in the fiscal burden from labor to capital. In jurisdictions with already high tax-to-GDP ratios, consolidation strategies may prioritize spending rationalization over further taxation. Conversely, countries facing acute fiscal slippage, like Romania, have implemented immediate VAT rate hikes, higher excise duties, and increased dividend taxes to swiftly curb aggregate demand and narrow fiscal gaps. Expenditure-first approaches, as seen in Slovakia, instead target VAT exemption reductions, market-value-based property taxes, and phased removal of temporary energy support to minimize growth drag.
- **Labor Market & Productivity Alignment:** Consolidation efforts are increasingly necessitated by demographic aging pressures and skills mismatches, requiring interventions to widen the income gap between work and non-work, limit unemployment benefits, promote lifelong learning, and improve product market regulation to offset sluggish productivity growth. Expanding high-quality early childhood education and care (ECEC) is identified as a critical lever to boost maternal labour force participation, alleviate poverty risks, and address structural skills shortages that constrain economic potential.
- **Preservation of Strategic Investment:** Emphasizes that consolidation must not indiscriminately cut public spending; priority is given to preserving and enhancing investments in infrastructure, education, green transition initiatives, and AI-driven innovation to support long-term growth and climate objectives.
- **Macro-Policy Synchronization & Financial Stability:** Fiscal consolidation is increasingly analyzed in tandem with monetary and financial sector policies. For example, credible fiscal adjustment can create necessary fiscal space for monetary easing when inflation aligns with targets. In practice, central banks like the [[National Bank of Romania]] have adopted cautious stances (e.g., maintaining benchmark rates at 6.5% and pausing cuts) until inflation and wage growth show sustained moderation, while also advocating for gradual exchange rate flexibility to enhance external resilience. Consolidation frameworks must also address financial sector distortions, including the sovereign-bank nexus, unhedged foreign exchange corporate loans, and buoyant consumer credit growth, ensuring fiscal space is not undermined by financial stability risks.
- **Implementation & Political Risks:** Consolidation pathways face elevated risks due to domestic political dynamics, potential revenue shortfalls, public resistance to austerity measures, and external shocks. Successful execution requires coordinated industrial policies, clear communication of medium-term targets, adaptive risk management for geopolitical uncertainties and trade barriers, and vigilance against upside inflation risks driven by domestic wage pressures and energy price volatility.
- **Ageing Costs & Social Fairness Pressures:** Demographic shifts and rising healthcare and pension expenditures frequently push structural deficits above conventional thresholds (e.g., exceeding 4% of GDP in several EU member states). Consolidation strategies must therefore integrate targeted tax reforms to shift burdens away from labour, strengthen social safety nets for vulnerable groups (children, single parents, migrants), and address rising housing affordability pressures to maintain social cohesion during adjustment periods.
- **Innovation, Regulation & SME Financing:** High regulatory burdens and complex permitting procedures can stifle business dynamism and productivity growth, undermining consolidation efforts. Modernizing professional services regulations, streamlining administrative processes, and expanding access to risk financing and venture capital for SMEs are increasingly viewed as essential components of fiscal consolidation to unlock innovation, improve the innovation-to-productivity translation gap, and foster sustainable economic recovery.
- **Green Transition & Industrial Competitiveness:** Fiscal adjustment must carefully balance environmental objectives with industrial competitiveness. While phasing out environmentally harmful fossil fuel subsidies is a priority, consolidation frameworks must mitigate risks associated with high industrial energy prices and grid expansion delays. Accelerated permitting for net-zero industries, diversification of energy supplies, and increased investment in circular economy infrastructure are critical to ensuring that green transitions support rather than hinder macroeconomic stabilization.
