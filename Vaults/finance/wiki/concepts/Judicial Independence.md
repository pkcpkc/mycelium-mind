# Judicial Independence

## Summary

Judicial Independence is a fundamental component of the Rule of Law, ensuring that courts and judicial bodies function without undue influence from political or other external actors. According to the EU Commission's 2025 Rule of Law Reports, perceived judicial independence varies significantly across member states. While Austria and Belgium maintain high perceived independence among the public and businesses, Malta also reports high perception levels. France and Italy report average perception, whereas Hungary reports consistently low perception. Poland's perceived judicial independence remains critically low, though substantial progress has been made in implementing the Rule of Law Action Plan. Slovakia's perceived judicial independence remains low among both the general public and businesses, with systemic implementation gaps persisting despite high-level commitments to reform. Across these jurisdictions, key challenges involve administrative court appointment procedures, prosecutorial autonomy, chronic case backlogs, digitalisation oversight, judicial council safeguards, dissolution of anti-corruption bodies, and deepening institutional crises. Recent legislative initiatives in France, Austria, Belgium, Hungary, Italy, Malta, Poland, and Slovakia aim to enhance judicial budgetary autonomy, streamline appointment mechanisms, modernise case management, strengthen judicial council safeguards, and resolve long-standing institutional disputes.

## Key Details

- **Perception vs. Standards:** Perceived judicial independence is rated highly in Austria, Belgium, and Malta, whereas France and Italy report average perception among the public and businesses. Hungary stands out with consistently low perception levels among citizens and businesses, while Poland's perceived independence remains critically low. Slovakia also reports low perceived judicial independence among the general public and businesses. Despite favorable perceptions in some jurisdictions, systemic implementation often falls short of full European standards in institutional mechanisms and practical efficiency.
- **Judicial Appointments & Governance:** Austria’s appointment process for administrative court presidents lacks consistent judicial involvement. France’s **[[Law of 20 November 2023]]** reformed the status of magistrates by repealing age limits and introducing 360° evaluations for court presidents. The **[[Superior Council of Magistracy]]** in France has actively defended judicial independence, particularly against political or personal attacks following national elections. In Hungary, the 2023 judicial reforms and the subsequent Fourteenth Amendment to the Fundamental Law altered appointment criteria, raised the minimum age for judges, and extended mandatory retirement to age 70. These changes sparked protests from judicial associations citing compromised independence. Transparency in case allocation has not improved in lower courts, though the **[[Kúria]]** continues to manage it effectively. Italy’s High Council for the Judiciary is executing new oversight mandates following comprehensive reforms, and a proposed constitutional separation of judges and prosecutors currently faces parliamentary review alongside judicial pushback. Malta's parliament is engaged in ongoing discussions regarding a constitutional reform to formally involve the judiciary in the appointment process of the Chief Justice, aiming to further insulate the role from executive influence. In Poland, the government is advancing draft legislation to resolve the status of judges appointed under the post-2017 **[[National Council for the Judiciary]]**, a process closely monitored by the **[[Venice Commission]]** to ensure proportionality and judicial review. A deepening institutional crisis persists with the **[[Constitutional Tribunal]]**, as the executive and legislative branches refuse to recognize its legitimacy, publish its rulings, or appoint new judges, triggering an ongoing infringement procedure at the **[[Court of Justice of the EU]]**.
- **Slovakia's Institutional Challenges:** Proposed amendments to judges' laws aim to improve court efficiency and introduce a second-instance review for disciplinary decisions, though concerns persist regarding the **[[Judicial Council]]**'s expanded role and insufficient safeguards for dismissing its non-judge members. The power of the **[[Prosecutor General]]** to annul final prosecutorial decisions continues to raise independence concerns. This is compounded by the dissolution of specialised anti-corruption bodies like the **[[Special Prosecutor's Office]]** and **[[National Crime Agency]],** which has caused investigation delays and a notable drop in corruption cases. Additionally, the frequent use of **[[Fast-Track Legislation]]** systematically bypasses meaningful stakeholder consultation, while changes to statutes of limitations have reduced penalties for high-level offences. Amendments to the **[[Freedom of Information Act]]** and a narrowing **[[Civil Society]]** environment further strain institutional oversight and accountability.
- **Prosecutorial Independence & Structural Transfers:** Progress toward an independent Federal Prosecution Office in Austria remains limited, with prosecutors currently subject to individual instructions by the Minister of Justice. Belgium’s anti-corruption structures maintain operational cooperation but face trust challenges regarding information leaks and potential political interference in high-profile cases. Hungary has relaxed selection criteria for the Prosecutor General, heightening risks of political influence over the prosecution service. In Malta, the transfer of non-summary prosecutions from the Police to the Office of the Attorney General has been completed, yet the structural shift has not fully resolved systemic pressures, as severe resource shortages continue to impact prosecutorial efficiency. Separately, Poland has achieved significant progress toward prosecutorial independence by officially separating the office of the Minister of Justice from that of the Prosecutor General, marking a pivotal structural reform in its anti-corruption and judicial landscape.
- **Capacity, Backlogs & Resources:** The Austrian judiciary requires an estimated 200 additional judicial posts to manage workload pressures. Belgium faces chronic backlogs, particularly at the Court of Appeal of Brussels, alongside structural resource deficiencies. France continues to face efficiency concerns with high average times to resolve cases, prompting the Ministry of Justice to launch "flash missions" to reduce backlogs and improve sentence execution. In Hungary, salary increases for judicial staff are being implemented in staggered steps until 2027, but structural safeguards and adequate resources remain absent. Italy continues to face persistent staffing shortages for magistrates and technical hurdles that delay the full digitalisation of criminal courts, though ongoing recruitment efforts aim to address these gaps. Malta similarly grapples with severe resource shortages that drive lengthy proceedings and significant case backlogs, underscoring the need for sustained investment in judicial capacity.
- **Digitalisation & Oversight:** Austria’s Justice 3.0 initiative and France’s **[[Portalis]]** tool are driving digitalisation efforts to improve case management and budgetary autonomy. However, implementation speed, technical issues, and oversight gaps persist; Belgium’s digitalisation has faced Court of Audit criticism for fraud risks, while France's digital rollout requires ongoing technical stabilization. Italy's criminal court digitalisation is similarly stalled by technical hurdles and resource constraints, alongside broader efficiency concerns. Malta is actively implementing digitalisation initiatives to modernise court processes and improve case management, though integration with existing legacy systems remains a focus area for ongoing technical oversight.
- **Tribunal Independence & Accountability Mechanisms:** Specialised tribunals and investigative bodies require robust safeguards to prevent political encroachment. In Malta, reforms aimed at addressing the independence of specialised tribunals have been initiated following critical **[[European Court of Human Rights]]** rulings. However, a recent legislative reform increasing the threshold for launching magisterial inquiries has drawn strong criticism from legal experts and civil society for potentially hindering accountability in high-level offences, raising concerns about checks on executive and judicial power. In Poland, plans to dismantle the controversial Chamber of Extraordinary Control and Public Affairs aim to bolster the independence of the **[[Supreme Court]]**, reflecting broader efforts to recalibrate judicial authority and restore institutional balance.
- **ECtHR Compliance & Legal Safeguards:** Procedural safeguards and legal professional privilege remain under scrutiny, with debates over lawyer-client communications and potential indirect interference. Hungary faces ongoing challenges in implementing **[[European Court of Human Rights]]** judgments, with over 50 leading judgments still pending execution. Additionally, undue pressure on judges persists in several jurisdictions, exemplified by disciplinary actions against judicial critics in Hungary and broader concerns over political interference. Italy's Constitutional Court has validated recent tax court adjustments, but accountability gaps persist regarding the lagging implementation of European Court of Human Rights judgments and ongoing Council of Europe supervision over proceeding lengths. Malta continues to face significant compliance challenges, with over two-thirds of recent **[[European Court of Human Rights]]** judgments still pending execution, highlighting systemic delays in domestic legal adaptation and enforcement.
