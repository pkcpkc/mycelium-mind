# 2025 Country Report Belgium

The European Commission's 2025 assessment highlights Belgium navigating a period of moderate economic deceleration amid structural fiscal and social challenges. Economic growth slowed to 1% in 2024 and is projected at 0.8% for 2025, driven by weakening private consumption and exports following external shocks. **[[Public Finances]]** are deteriorating, with the headline deficit projected to reach 5.4% of GDP in 2025 and public debt expected to climb toward 126.4% by 2035 without policy intervention. A primary driver is the substantial rise in **[[Ageing Costs]]**, particularly pension and long-term care expenditures, which are forecast to surge significantly through 2070. The **[[Fiscal Framework]]** itself requires strengthening, as Belgium remains the only EU member without a fully developed multiannual fiscal planning system at the national level, risking deviations from medium-term trajectories and undermining institutional credibility.

To address these pressures, the report outlines an urgent need for **[[Tax and Benefits System]]** reforms. The current design creates strong disincentives to work, characterized by high labour taxes, an unemployment trap, and non-means-tested benefits that prolong inactivity. The federal government plans to limit unemployment benefits to two years, shift the tax burden away from labour, and reform the pension system via a bonus-malus mechanism to improve long-term sustainability. Simultaneously, **[[Business Dynamism]]** remains subdued due to high regulatory burdens, restrictive service sectors, and concentrated **[[Innovation Diffusion]]** where R&D spending (3.3% of GDP) benefits only a few large firms rather than spilling over to SMEs. High energy costs and skilled labour shortages further constrain competitiveness and investment, particularly in technical and STEM fields.

On the sustainability front, Belgium faces significant hurdles in meeting its 2030 climate targets. The manufacturing sector accounts for nearly 30% of national GHG emissions, and industrial decarbonisation lags due to high electricity prices relative to gas, driven by heavy taxation and levies. The **[[Renewable Energy Roll-out]]** is hampered by grid congestion, lengthy permitting procedures, and public acceptance issues. Despite progress in circular economy practices and water management, diffuse agricultural pollution and biodiversity loss remain critical concerns. Fossil fuel subsidies persist without a clear phase-out timeline, undermining electrification incentives and energy transition goals.

Socially, the **[[Labour Market]]** exhibits paradoxical dynamics: while employment reached a record 72.3%, it remains below the EU average due to low activity rates, particularly among low-skilled workers, migrants, and older adults. High labour shortages coexist with weak activation policies and significant **[[Social Fairness]]** disparities across regions, with Brussels and Wallonia facing higher poverty and exclusion rates than Flanders. Educational inequalities are pronounced, featuring declining student performance, early tracking mechanisms, and teacher shortages, which exacerbate skill mismatches for the green and digital transitions. The report concludes that Belgium must accelerate RRP implementation, streamline governance across federal and regional levels, and prioritize targeted reforms to align fiscal sustainability with inclusive growth and climate resilience.
