# 2025 Rule of Law Report: Country Chapter on Poland

This report evaluates Poland's rule of law landscape across four key pillars, highlighting significant legislative efforts amidst persistent structural challenges. In the justice system, perceived judicial independence remains critically low, though substantial progress has been made in implementing the [[Rule of Law Action Plan]]. The government is advancing draft legislation to resolve the status of judges appointed under the post-2017 [[National Council for the Judiciary]], a process closely monitored by the [[Venice Commission]] to ensure proportionality and judicial review. Plans to dismantle the controversial Chamber of Extraordinary Control and Public Affairs aim to bolster the independence of the [[Supreme Court]]. However, a deepening institutional crisis persists with the [[Constitutional Tribunal]], as the executive and legislative branches refuse to recognize its legitimacy, publish its rulings, or appoint new judges, triggering an ongoing infringement procedure at the Court of Justice of the EU. Conversely, separating the office of the Minister of Justice from that of the Prosecutor General marks significant progress toward prosecutorial independence.

The anti-corruption framework shows mixed results. While the perception of corruption remains high, Poland has fully operationalized the [[European Public Prosecutor Office]] and is restructuring anti-corruption competences by dissolving the Central Anti-Corruption Bureau and redistributing its functions to police and security agencies. Large-scale corruption cases are being actively investigated, yet no progress has been made on introducing lobbying regulations or standardizing digital asset declarations. Impunity clauses and broad immunities for top executives continue to hinder accountability. Weaknesses in political party financing persist, and [[Public Procurement]] remains a high-risk sector for graft, despite some successful investigations into bid-rigging and embezzlement. A new whistleblower protection law has been enacted, offering a vital channel for reporting irregularities.

Regarding media pluralism and freedom, the government is drafting reforms to implement the European Media Freedom Act, aiming to restore transparency and independence to broadcasting regulators and streamline licensing procedures. Politically biased content in public service media has notably decreased, though editorial independence is still debated amid the liquidation of state broadcasters and the temporary suspension of licence fee funding. The state plans to transition to direct budget funding for public broadcasting. Challenges remain regarding media ownership transparency and the safeguarding of journalists, with a decrease in [[Strategic Lawsuits Against Public Participation]] and ongoing legislative work to implement anti-SLAPP directives and protect journalistic sources.

Finally, broader institutional checks and balances have seen improvements. Legislative consultation rules have been strengthened to enhance law-making quality, and the civic space rating has improved from "obstructed" to "narrowed." The Ombudsperson continues to play a pivotal role in safeguarding fundamental rights. However, the implementation of European Court of Human Rights judgments remains sluggish, and investigative committees examining past abuses of power face constitutional scrutiny. Overall, Poland demonstrates a trajectory of active reform and institutional recalibration, though deep-seated rule of law concerns require sustained political commitment and full alignment with EU standards.

(source: [EU Commission 2025 Rule of Law Report](https://commission.europa.eu/publications/2025-rule-law-report_en))
