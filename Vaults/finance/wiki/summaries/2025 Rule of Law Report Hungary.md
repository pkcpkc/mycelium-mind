# 2025 Rule of Law Report: Hungary

The European Commission’s 2025 assessment highlights persistent systemic challenges in Hungary’s **[[Rule of Law Mechanism]]**, with minimal progress across all monitored pillars. In the **[[Judicial Independence]]** sector, perceived independence remains low among citizens and businesses. The 2023 judicial reforms and the subsequent Fourteenth Amendment to the Fundamental Law altered judge appointment criteria, raised the minimum age for judges, and allowed service until 70, sparking protests from judicial associations over compromised independence. While salary increases are being implemented in staggered steps until 2027, structural safeguards remain absent. Case allocation transparency in lower courts has not improved, though the *Kúria* manages it effectively. Undue pressure on judges persists, exemplified by disciplinary actions against critics and the relaxation of Prosecutor General selection criteria, which heightens risks of political influence over the prosecution service.

Regarding the **[[Anti-Corruption Framework]]**, public and expert perception of corruption remains high, with a decrease in final convictions for corruption crimes. The implementation of the 2024-2025 National Anti-Corruption Strategy shows ongoing efforts but lacks a robust track record for high-level cases. The Integrity Authority continues to face operational obstacles in accessing data and fulfilling oversight tasks. Legislative reforms on lobbying, revolving doors, and asset declarations remain pending or insufficient. Transparency in political party financing and electoral campaigns continues to be a critical gap. While competition in public procurement has slightly improved, significant risks persist due to fragmented oversight and preferential treatment for state-aligned entities.

**[[Media Pluralism]]** and media freedom show no measurable progress. The National Media and Infocommunications Authority lacks functional independence, and state advertising continues to heavily favor pro-government outlets. Public service media remains financially dependent on the governing majority and subject to political influence. Journalists face increased pressure, including smear campaigns, selective denial of press access, and investigations by the Sovereignty Protection Office under allegations of serving "foreign interests." Access to information remains restricted, particularly concerning public funding and subcontractors.

Institutional **[[Checks and Balances]]** remain weak, with the government extensively utilizing emergency powers to bypass parliamentary oversight, creating legal uncertainty for businesses and foreign investors. The Constitutional Court’s independence is questioned due to its political appointment process and role in reviewing ordinary court decisions. Civil society **[[Civil Society Space]]** is rated as obstructed, with the Sovereignty Protection Office targeting independent NGOs and a proposed "transparency" law threatening foreign funding. Furthermore, Hungary’s implementation of European Court of Human Rights judgments continues to lag, with over 50 leading judgments pending. The Commission reiterates multiple recommendations for Hungary to enhance judicial transparency, establish high-level anti-corruption track records, ensure media independence, and foster a safe civic space.
