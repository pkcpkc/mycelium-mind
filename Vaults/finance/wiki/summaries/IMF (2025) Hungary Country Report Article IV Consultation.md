# IMF (2025) Hungary Country Report: Article IV Consultation

The [[Hungary]] economy is navigating a challenging juncture characterized by output stagnation over the past three years and persistent [[Inflation]] well above the [[Magyar Nemzeti Bank]]'s 3 percent target. Regulatory interventions, including price and margin caps, windfall taxes, and subsidized lending schemes, have distorted market signals and contributed to macroeconomic imbalances. Despite notable fiscal consolidation in recent years, public debt remains elevated at approximately 73.5 percent of GDP, with financing costs and high risk premia constraining growth potential.

The near-term outlook projects a modest consumption-driven recovery, with GDP growth expected to reach 0.7 percent in 2025 and accelerate to 2 percent in 2026, supported by favorable wage dynamics, a nascent investment recovery, and positive spillovers from German fiscal expansion. [[Inflation]] is forecast to decelerate gradually, reaching 4.5 percent by Q4 2025 and converging to the central bank's target by 2027. However, significant downside risks persist, including deepening geoeconomic fragmentation, potential delays in [[EU Funds]] disbursements due to governance and rule-of-law conditionality, and the fiscal impact of upcoming national elections.

To restore macroeconomic stability and foster sustainable growth, the [[IMF]] staff and Executive Board emphasize several critical policy adjustments. [[Fiscal Policy]] must prioritize high-quality adjustment to rebuild buffers, targeting a structural primary surplus of approximately 1.75 percent of GDP by the medium term. Recommended measures include broadening the tax base, phasing out distortive retail energy subsidies in favor of targeted cash transfers, rationalizing the wage bill and public administration, and implementing reforms to contain long-term pension and healthcare expenditures. Additionally, enhanced monitoring of state-owned enterprises and off-balance-sheet contingent liabilities is essential to mitigate fiscal risks.

[[Monetary Policy]] should maintain a tight stance through 2026 to durably anchor inflation expectations, leveraging a data-dependent approach amid high uncertainty. The [[IMF]] strongly recommends phasing out retail margin caps and voluntary interest rate ceilings, which distort market pricing and undermine monetary transmission. Exchange rate flexibility and robust reserve coverage should continue to serve as critical shock absorbers.

The financial sector remains broadly well-capitalized and liquid, but supervisory vigilance is warranted regarding vulnerabilities in the corporate sector, rising sovereign-bank nexus exposures, commercial real estate valuations, and buoyant housing prices driven by subsidized lending. Macroprudential measures should be calibrated strictly on financial stability grounds, and borrower-based limits should avoid differentiation motivated by non-stability objectives like housing affordability.

[[Structural Reforms]] are identified as crucial for closing Hungary's productivity gap and enhancing competitiveness. Priority areas include streamlining insolvency frameworks to foster firm dynamism, expanding access to risk capital for high-growth firms, accelerating the green transition and energy diversification to reduce external vulnerabilities, and advancing governance and rule-of-law reforms. Unlocking suspended [[EU Funds]] is explicitly linked to these governance improvements, which would significantly bolster external financing and investment confidence.

The Hungarian authorities broadly concur with the staff's assessment, emphasizing their commitment to targeted stability measures, ongoing fiscal consolidation, and structural investments in strategic sectors. They project stronger medium-term growth driven by recent capital expenditures and express confidence that current policy frameworks will sustain macrofinancial stability while addressing social and housing priorities.
