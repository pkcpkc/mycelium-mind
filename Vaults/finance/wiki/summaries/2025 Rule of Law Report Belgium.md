# 2025 Rule of Law Report Belgium

The European Commission's 2025 Rule of Law Report provides a comprehensive assessment of Belgium’s institutional and legal landscape, highlighting a mixed trajectory of progress and persistent challenges. Overall, `[[Judicial Independence]]` remains highly perceived by both the general public and businesses, with the government maintaining its commitment to transferring budgetary management powers from the executive to the judiciary. However, `[[Justice System Efficiency]]` continues to be hampered by chronic backlogs, particularly at the Court of Appeal of Brussels, and structural resource deficiencies. While recent measures, such as publishing all magistrate vacancies and implementing a file management project funded by the Recovery and Resilience Facility, mark incremental progress, long-term structural reforms are still required. The digitalisation of the justice system also faces criticism from the Court of Audit for inadequate oversight and fraud risks, though initial corrective action plans have been submitted. Legal professional privilege debates continue following directives restricting lawyer-client recordings, prompting calls for additional procedural safeguards.

In the domain of integrity and governance, Belgium’s `[[Anti-Corruption Framework]]` shows relatively low perceived corruption levels among experts and citizens, yet enforcement gaps endure. The Central Office for the Repression of Corruption (CDBC-OCRC) maintains operational cooperation with prosecutors, but allegations of information leaks and potential political interference in high-profile cases have raised public trust concerns. New investigative structures targeting financial crime and organised crime are planned under a “follow-the-money” strategy. However, `[[Asset Declarations]]` lack independent verification and transparency mechanisms, remaining accessible only during criminal investigations. Legislative reform on `[[Lobbying Reform]]` has stalled, with the new government yet to decide on draft rules. Progress on `[[Revolving Doors]]` regulations and `[[Whistleblower Protection]]` remains limited, particularly regarding the scope of parliamentary whistleblower laws and the absence of binding cooling-off periods for senior officials. Integrity policies across local police zones are fragmented, prompting plans for career-long integrity screening and addressing high risks of corruption linked to public procurement.

`[[Media Pluralism]]` and press freedom are structurally supported, with regulators in Flanders, the French-speaking Community, and the German-speaking Community maintaining independence while adapting to EU mandates like the Digital Services Act and European Media Freedom Act. Public service broadcasters (VRT, RTBF, BRF) face governance complexities and budgetary constraints, notably RTBF’s significant funding freeze. Legislative updates have improved access to public documents, though procedural fragmentation and non-binding appeal bodies limit effectiveness. Legal safeguards for journalists have been strengthened through the decriminalisation of defamation and proposed anti-SLAPP transposition, yet journalists report increasing harassment, political interference, and intimidation online and during demonstrations.

Regarding institutional checks and balances, compliance by public authorities with final rulings from national courts and the European Court of Human Rights remains inadequate, triggering ongoing Council of Europe supervision. The Federal Human Rights Institute has flagged risks associated with proposed budget cuts to equality bodies and called for adherence to the Paris Principles. Meanwhile, `[[Civic Space]]` faces emerging pressures, with proposed judicial bans on demonstrations for individuals convicted of protest-related offences drawing constitutional and human rights scrutiny. Despite these challenges, business confidence in investment protection and the independence of the national competition authority remains relatively high. The report concludes with recommendations for sustained efforts in judicial resourcing, anti-corruption transparency, lobbying regulation, and strict adherence to European judicial standards.
